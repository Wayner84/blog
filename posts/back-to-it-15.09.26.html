Back to it

15.09.26

It’s been absolutely ages since I updated this blog. I’ve been doing a bit rubbish with my mental health, so I’ve neglected to record what I’ve been up to. I have still been busy though!

Today was my first day back on the HNC Electrical and Electronic Engineering course. This year is much closer to what I’m interested in: more electrical content and a lot more technical detail. It’s still fairly basic at the moment, but I’m really looking forward to getting into PLC control systems and learning how they work in industry.

I’m so used to microcontrollers that I assumed they were the standard for this sort of thing. I was very wrong. There’s a lot to learn and plenty of new software to get used to, but that’s all good learning. Good God, I’m glad to be back. The course gives me a sense of purpose and the motivation to keep going with my personal projects as well.

The Debian server

I wasn’t too happy hosting everything on the main system I use for gaming and heavy computation. Running multiple Docker containers and storing an ever-growing number of files on it was becoming infeasible, especially with absolutely zero backups in place!

You already know what I’m going to say… 3, 2, 1 backups: three copies, across at least two types of storage, with one copy off-site.

That was the main reason for building a separate server from spare components. It’s only a crappy FX-6300 with 24 GB of DDR3 RAM and an old AMD graphics card for display output, since the FX-6300 has no integrated graphics. It isn’t the beefiest machine in the world, but I’ve connected about 2.5 TB of storage across several drives.

I’ve set the drives up with SnapRAID and one full parity drive. Once parity is synced, that gives me protection against one data drive failing, provided the remaining drives and parity data are healthy. I’m planning to increase that to two parity drives soon, mainly because I can’t stop worrying about it.

SnapRAID parity isn’t the same thing as having a full backup, and I’m not keeping a proper copy off-site yet. I do keep the most important things on an external hard drive that stays in my bag. It’s not ideal, but it’s something while I work towards a better setup.

A server without a case

The whole system is currently sitting on top of one of my plastic drawer units with no case, which is obviously not ideal.

I’ve printed enclosures for the drives: six slots for 3.5-inch drives and four for 2.5-inch drives. I still need to assemble everything and build a shell for the actual PC, with the hard-drive enclosures sitting on top so it looks and works more like a proper little server.

Anyway, I digress.

Moving everything off my main PC

Performance is substantially better now. My books are stored in BookLore on the server, my images are in Immich, and my 3D files, Jotty notes and plenty of other things have moved across too.

That includes my Minecraft Bedrock server, which has made a big difference in-game. I don’t get the stutters I had before, and when my friend and I are playing together it runs without the issues we used to get.

Organising the hoard

One thing that becomes very obvious when you start hoarding files is how difficult they are to organise. Old filing systems stop making sense, projects change, and a folder structure that worked six months ago can become completely inappropriate. Trying to keep on top of that manually is a nightmare.

This is where Hermes Agent and Codex have become incredibly useful. I can ask Hermes to audit a location and come back with a comprehensive plan for how the documents should be stored, which folders make sense and what is sitting in the wrong place. After I approve the plan, it can reorganise everything and verify the result.

I try to give the main subfolders to Hermes for another look roughly once a month. Quite often it finds something in the wrong location and moves it somewhere more appropriate. I’m still fascinated by how well it works, and it has made managing a growing pile of files much easier.

Karpathy’s LLM Wiki

That leads me on to another thing: Karpathy’s LLM Wiki concept.

I first came across the idea a good few months ago. The basic methodology lets an AI agent retain useful information in an organised knowledge base without cramming all of it into the context for every single prompt. Hermes manages the wiki itself, deciding what is worth retaining and keeping the links between related notes up to date.

After a while it starts to form a sort of second brain. Mine has reached the point where I can see distinct groups forming around different parts of my life and projects. Anything related to PC-DMIS sits together, away from things such as my custom PC case, while still linking across when there’s a genuine connection.

Since using it, the quality of the responses has been phenomenally better. Hermes retains important context, updates what it knows as projects change, and can improve its reusable skills based on the work we do and the feedback I give it. Most of that knowledge management happens without me having to think about it, and I can’t wait to see how large and useful the wiki becomes.

For me, this is what separates Hermes from being just another ChatGPT-style agent. It’s becoming something much more personal and useful: an assistant that can remember the work, understand how the pieces fit together and pick things back up without starting from scratch every time.

So, that’s where I’ve been. College is back, the server is running, the files are slowly becoming less chaotic, and I’ve got my motivation back. Hopefully it won’t be quite so long before the next update.
